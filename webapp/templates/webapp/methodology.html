Prediction Methodology

Last reviewed: July 10, 2026

How we forecast U.S. Visa Bulletin priority-date movement — what the model actually does, what the ranges mean, where the data comes from, and how accurate it really is.

We publish this so you can judge the forecasts for yourself. These are model estimates, not guarantees — read the accuracy section and caveats before you rely on any date.

What the forecast is

Every month the U.S. Department of State publishes cut-off dates in the Visa Bulletin. Our Bulletin Forecast Model estimates where those cut-off dates are likely to move next, per category (EB-1 through EB-5, F1 through F4) and per country of chargeability (India, China, Mexico, Philippines, and Rest of World). It runs on more than a decade of bulletin history plus demand signals from published immigration data.

Two models, two horizons

Priority-date movement behaves very differently over a one-month horizon than over a year, so we use two approaches:

  • Near-term (next bulletin). The model first classifies each visa series into a regime — advancing, stalled, or retrogressing — from its recent movement, then applies the strategy that has historically been most accurate for that state. A series that has been stalled for months is forecast differently from one advancing steadily.
  • Longer horizon (roughly 6–12 months). A gradient-boosted model combines historical bulletin patterns, pending demand, and fiscal-year cycles to catch structural turns — like the annual reset on October 1 — that simple month-to-month extrapolation misses.

What the ranges mean

Where we show a range around a predicted date, it is an 80% range: based on how the model has performed historically for that kind of series and horizon, we'd expect the actual cut-off to land inside that band about 80% of the time. A wider band means the model is less certain — usually a volatile or thin-data series. It is a statement about uncertainty, not a promise; unusual months fall outside it.

Where the data comes from

  • Visa Bulletin history — parsed automatically from U.S. Department of State bulletins. New bulletins are ingested within about a day of release, and the forecast refreshes with them.
  • Employment & wage data — public disclosure data from the U.S. Department of Labor's Office of Foreign Labor Certification (H-1B / LCA and PERM filings), used for demand context and for the salary features.

Everything is extracted programmatically from official government files. There is no manual data entry.

How accurate is it?

We deliberately do not advertise a single headline “X% accurate” number, because it would be misleading. Accuracy depends enormously on the category, the country, and how far out you look:

  • For stable, well-behaved series the near-term forecast is often within a few weeks of the actual cut-off.
  • For volatile series — heavily backlogged categories, or periods around retrogression and the fiscal-year reset — errors can be far larger, sometimes several months.
  • The honest baseline: for many series in calm periods, a naive “assume it keeps doing what it just did” guess is already hard to beat. Our model's value is concentrated in the turns — regime changes and structural shifts — and in giving you a calibrated range rather than a false-precision single date.

We measure this by backtesting: for each past month we re-run the model using only the data that existed at the time, then compare its prediction to what the bulletin actually did. A detailed, per-category predicted-vs-actual track record is being published as its own page; until then, treat every forecast as an informed estimate with real uncertainty. Caveats

  • Policy beats models. A visa-number reallocation, a sudden demand surge, or a court decision can invalidate any forecast instantly. No historical model can see a policy change coming.
  • Dates are estimates, not legal advice. Do not make filing, travel, or job decisions on a forecast alone — confirm against the official bulletin and, where it matters, an attorney.
  • Salaries are offered wages. Wage figures come from what employers listed on visa filings, not W-2 income or total compensation (equity and bonuses are excluded).
